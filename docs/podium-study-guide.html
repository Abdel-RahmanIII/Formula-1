The project in four sentences

We predict whether a driver finishes on the podium (positionOrder <= 3) using only what is known after qualifying and before the start. The Ergast data (14 tables, 1950–2024) is audited and cleaned to one row per driver per race (24,482 rows), and 21 leakage-tested features are built in six groups. Five predictors are compared on a season-based split (train ≤ 2019, validation 2020–2021, test 2022–2024), and LightGBM is carried forward to ablation, SHAP/LIME and a predict_podium() function with calibrated probabilities. On the test seasons the learned models reach a PR-AUC of 0.707–0.720 against 0.647 for the “grid ≤ 3” rule, and none of them is measurably better than plain logistic regression.

Shared questions

  1. What is the target, and what may a feature use?

    podium = 1 when positionOrder <= 3. The prediction is made after qualifying and before the start, so a feature may use the grid slot, the qualifying session and anything from earlier races. It may not use anything from the race itself or standings updated after it.

    Cell 1 · DEC-004

  2. Why is the data split by season and not at random?
    • It matches use: train on the past, predict a race that has not happened.
    • Rows of one race are not independent. Exactly three drivers per race reach the podium, so a random split would let the model learn a race's outcome from its neighbours in training.
    • Regulations change by era. Keeping 2022 (new ground-effect cars) out of training tests whether the model carries over to new rules.

    Task 15 · DEC-002

  3. Why is PR-AUC the main metric, and not accuracy or ROC-AUC?

    Only about 15% of rows are podiums. Accuracy rewards predicting “no podium” everywhere (85% correct, useless). ROC-AUC also rewards ranking the many easy back-of-grid negatives, so every model sits around 0.93 and differences hide. PR-AUC (average precision) measures how well the few positives are ranked at the top, without picking a threshold. F1 at a tuned threshold is the secondary metric.

    Task 21 · DEC-022

  4. Where could leakage enter this pipeline, and what stops it?
    • Outcome columns as features: a name-based blacklist and regex guard (Section 1.3), checked in the data contract.
    • History features that include the current race: sort by date and shift(1), proven by a brute-force recomputation and a corruption test (Task 13).
    • Standings recorded after the race: use the previous round's table (Task 12.5), with an assertion that it predates the race.
    • Shared drives: one driver's grid paired with another's result, so those rows are removed (DEC-034).
    • Preprocessing statistics: the imputer and scaler are fitted on training rows only (Task 19 measures the alternative).
    • Model selection: everything is chosen on validation; the test seasons are scored once, in Task 28.
  5. What is the headline result?

    Test PR-AUC (2022–2024, 68 races): grid ≤ 3 rule 0.647, logistic regression 0.712, random forest 0.707, LightGBM 0.709, neural network 0.720.

    • The extra features are worth about 0.06 PR-AUC over the grid rule: the rule is 0.064 below logistic regression (95% interval −0.118 to −0.011).
    • Model complexity adds nothing measurable: every learned model's difference to logistic regression has an interval that includes zero.
    • Most of the signal is the starting position (ablation and SHAP); past results add about 0.03–0.06 on top; context adds nothing.

    Tasks 28, 31, 33

  6. Which model was selected, and was that the right call?

    LightGBM, by a rule fixed in advance: highest validation PR-AUC (0.743). On test it scores 0.709, behind the network (0.720) and level with logistic regression (0.712), all within noise. The choice is not revisited, because picking a model from test scores would turn the test seasons into a second validation set. Logistic regression would be an equally defensible and simpler choice; LightGBM was also convenient because it takes raw features with missing values and works with SHAP's exact TreeExplainer.

    Tasks 27.2, 28 · DEC-029, DEC-030

  7. What is the biggest limitation of the work?

    Any pre-race model has a ceiling: crashes, safety cars, penalties, weather and strategy happen during the race and are not in the inputs. The 2020 Italian GP cases in Task 34 show the model reading its inputs correctly and the outcome still going the other way. Beyond that: validation and test are small (39 and 68 races), so learned models cannot be separated from each other, and qualifying data is missing in about 63% of the training rows.

    Limitations, cells 203–204

  8. What is the decision log, and why does the notebook cite DEC numbers?

    docs/decision_log.md records every scope, cleaning, feature and modeling choice with its evidence and status (DEC-001 to DEC-037). Some decisions replace earlier ones after instructor rulings on Piazza: DEC-034 replaces DEC-008 (shared drives), DEC-035 replaces DEC-011 (Q1 uses the grid), DEC-036 replaces DEC-013 (tyre failures are mechanical). Know the replaced ones too; examiners ask why something changed.

Expected questions

  1. Why is the target built from positionOrder and not position?

    position is empty for every driver who was not classified (36.8% of clean rows). positionOrder gives every row a finishing order, so the target is defined for all rows. Both are blacklisted as features.

    Cell 1 · Task 8.6 shows the missing share

  2. How does the leakage guard work, and what can it not catch?

    assert_leakage_safe() raises a ValueError if a column is in LEAKAGE_BLACKLIST_COLUMNS (outcomes such as positionOrder, points, laps, milliseconds, statusId, post-race standings) or matches a pattern such as ^fastest, ^post_, current. A self-test at load time checks that five forbidden names are rejected.

    It only checks names. A feature with an innocent name computed from current-race data would pass, which is why Step 4 adds behavioural leakage tests (Person B).

    Cell 5 · DEC-004

  3. What is the data contract, and where is it enforced?

    One row per (raceId, driverId); raceId, driverId, constructorId, date, year never null; podium only 0 or 1; every feature passes the leakage guard. validate_data_contract() checks the grain after shared-drive removal (Task 6), the full contract on clean_df (Task 7.1) and on model_df (Task 12.8).

    Cell 6 · DEC-005

  4. How are missing values encoded in the raw files, and how do you know none survived?

    The CSVs write a missing value as the text \N. The loader reads \N and empty strings as NaN (na_values). Task 4.2 asserts no cleaned table still contains \N as text, and that parsing lap times lost no value that was present.

    Cells 3, 8, 27

  5. Why does all cleaning work on copies?

    So the raw tables stay available for the before/after EDA. Task 4.2 and Task 8.6 assert that the raw tables were not changed (raw races.date is still text, raw results has no pit_start).

    Cells 26–27, 43

  6. Which integrity checks did the audit run, and what did they find?
    • 16 primary keys, including composite ones such as (raceId, driverId, lap) for lap_times: all unique and non-null.
    • 22 foreign keys, checked by an anti-join (child keys not found in the parent): zero orphans.
    • One violation: results is not unique on (raceId, driverId). 176 rows in 85 pairs, 91 surplus rows, almost all in 1950–1964. These are shared drives.

    Task 2, cells 15–17

  7. What does qualifying coverage look like, and why does it matter later?

    qualifying.csv starts in 1994. From 1996 to 2002 it covers only 6–59% of the results rows; it is complete from 2003. Within the file, q2 is missing in 44% of rows and q3 in 65%, because the knockout format (2006 on) eliminates drivers before those sessions.

    Consequence: qualifying features are missing in about 63% of training rows, so they need a missing flag, and Step 5 fills quali_position from the grid.

    Tasks 1.4, 3.4, 8.5

  8. Why keep missing q2/q3 times instead of filling them?

    A driver knocked out in Q1 has no Q2 time by design. Filling it would invent a lap. The gap itself is information: it later becomes q3_reached.

    Task 4 intro, cell 25

  9. Why exclude the Indianapolis 500 (DEC-003)?

    From 1950 to 1960 it counted for the championship, but it was run under AAA/USAC rules on an oval, with a separate field that almost never raced in F1. Keeping it would distort front-row conversion and driver form. 11 races and 405 rows are removed; the cell asserts exactly one Indy 500 per season.

    Task 5.1, cell 29

  10. Explain the non-starter rule (DEC-009). Why not drop every row labelled “did not qualify”?

    A row is removed when the driver completed zero laps and either carries a non-start label (F, W, Did not qualify, Did not prequalify, Withdrew) or never held a grid slot (grid == 0).

    The label alone is not reliable: 24 of the 1,724 labelled rows completed racing laps, and 173 held a grid slot. The cell asserts that no removed row finished on the podium. 1,715 rows removed.

    Task 5.2, cell 30

  11. What is a pit-lane start, and how is it encoded (DEC-010)?

    After DEC-009, every remaining grid == 0 row completed laps (asserted), so it is a real pit-lane start. Left as 0, a model would read it as better than pole. So grid becomes the race's last grid slot + 1 and pit_start = 1. 72 rows, 2015–2024, none reached the podium.

    Task 5.3, cell 31

  12. What is a shared drive, and how was it resolved (DEC-034)?

    Until the mid-1960s a driver could take over a teammate's car mid-race. Ergast lists that car under both drivers with the same grid slot and classification. The slot belongs to whoever started the car and the result to whoever finished it, and the data does not say which is which. Pairing one driver's pre-race data with another's result is leakage (instructor ruling on Piazza).

    • A car is (raceId, constructorId, grid, positionOrder). Listed under two drivers, it was shared.
    • Every row of a shared car is removed. A driver who also drove a car of their own keeps that row (43 of 135). A driver left with two cars of their own is removed (1).
    • Same slot but different classification means two different cars (a data-entry collision, 29 rows) and is kept.
    • 155 shared-car rows in 40 races (1950–1964); 157 rows removed in total. Assertion: no shared car survives.

    Task 6, cells 32–33

  13. Why was the old shared-drive rule (DEC-008) dropped?

    DEC-008 kept each driver's best-classified row. 59 of the 61 rows it kept were shared cars, so it kept exactly the pairing ruled to be leakage. Example shown in the cell: the 1957 British GP, where Moss and Brooks are both listed with grid 3 and the win.

    Task 6 · decision log DEC-034

  14. Why is field_size computed before the shared rows are removed?

    It should count every driver who took the start. Counting after removal would undercount starters in the races that had shared cars.

    Task 6, cell 33

  15. Some races have fewer than three podium rows after cleaning. Is that a bug?

    No. When a podium car was shared, its rows are removed: 13 races keep two podium rows and 2 keep one; 1,099 races have three. The assertion is that no race has more than three.

    Task 7.2, cell 36

  16. Why rename qualifying.position and prefix the sprint columns?

    Both tables reuse names of the results outcome columns. Renaming to quali_position and sprint_* avoids merge collisions, and keeps the guard (which blacklists position) from rejecting a legitimate pre-race feature.

    Task 7.1, cell 35

  17. Walk through the row counts and the podium rate across cleaning.

    26,759 raw → 26,354 after Indy (−405) → 24,639 after non-starters (−1,715) → 24,482 after shared drives (−157). The podium rate rises from 12.69% to 13.58% because non-starters (never on the podium) are gone. It stays under 15% because older races had more than 20 starters; validation and test, with 20-car grids, are at 15.1%.

    Tasks 7.2, 8.1

  18. Why does clean_df still contain outcome columns?

    The Step 3 questions measure outcomes (podium, retirement status), so they need them. The columns stay blacklisted, and Step 4 selects only identifiers, the target and pre-race columns.

    Task 7 intro

  19. What stands out about 1989 in the EDA?

    It is the largest season in the raw data (620 rows) and has the lowest raw podium rate (7.74%), because pre-qualifying produced many non-starters. After cleaning, its rate rises the most of any season (+3.82 points). The largest field drops from 55 entries to 34 starters.

  1. Q1: how is a front-row start defined, and why the grid and not qualifying (DEC-035)?

    Grid slot 1 or 2, excluding pit-lane starts. The question is about the starting grid, and the instructors confirmed on Piazza that the note on qualifying only explains that penalties make the two differ. Using the grid also lets Q1 use every season, since qualifying data starts in 1994.

  2. Q1: what is the answer?
    • Conversion rose from 56.3% (991 of 1,759) in 1950–2013 to 76.2% (345 of 453) in 2014–2024. It rose at 14 of 16 ranked circuits.
    • Top since 2014: Monaco 89.5%, Suzuka 88.9%, Yas Marina 86.4%. Last: Hungaroring 59.1%.

    Tasks 9.2–9.3, Q1 answer cell 51

  3. Q1: why the minimum of 10 front-row starts per era, and how much can you trust single ranks?

    With fewer starts, one race moves a rate by 10 points or more. Even with the filter, hybrid-era circuits have 14–26 front-row starts, so one race moves a rate by 4–7 points. The overall rise is solid; most individual rank changes are within that noise.

    Task 9.2

  4. Q1: what does the sensitivity check with 2003–2013 show?

    1950–2013 mixes very different cars, and long-used circuits (Monza 127 front-row starts, Monaco 119) are dominated by old decades. With a pre-hybrid window as long as the hybrid era, the rise is smaller (67.0% → 76.2%) and the ranking is loosely kept (ρ = 0.36, 14 circuits). Same answer: the hybrid era converts more often and reorders the circuits.

    Task 9.2

  5. Why Wilson intervals, and why Spearman rather than Pearson?

    Wilson intervals stay inside 0–100% and behave well with small counts and rates near the edges, where the usual normal (Wald) interval breaks. Spearman compares ranks, which is what “does the ranking change” asks, and does not assume a linear relation.

    Cell 46, Task 9.2

  6. Q2: how is a home race defined (DEC-012), and which edge cases did you handle?

    A race is a home race when circuits.country is one of the countries mapped from drivers.nationality.

    • Names follow the circuits table: British → UK, American → USA; “United States” folded into USA.
    • Monegasque → Monaco, East German → Germany. Dual nationalities (American-Italian) have two home countries.
    • “Argentinian ” has a trailing space in the raw table and is stripped.
    • 14 nationalities (Finnish among them) have no circuit, so their drivers are always away. Every nationality is mapped (asserted).

    Task 10.1

  7. Q2: the raw home podium rate is lower than away. Why is that not a home disadvantage?

    Grid position confounds it. Home starts come from the first three slots less often (11.9% vs. 13.7%), and the raw rates are 11.57% at home vs. 13.75% away. Comparing within grid buckets (1, 2–3, 4–6, 7–10, 11+) removes that, and the differences then go both ways with overlapping intervals.

    Task 10.2

  8. Q2: which tests did you run and what do they say?
    • Cochran–Mantel–Haenszel over the 5 grid buckets: pooled odds ratio 0.91 (95% CI 0.76–1.08), p = 0.31.
    • Logistic regression podium ~ is_home + C(grid_bucket), errors clustered by driver: 0.91 (0.75–1.11), p = 0.35.
    • Robustness: exact grid slot 0.92, only drivers who ever had a home race 0.90, season decade added 0.91.
    • Breslow–Day p = 0.32: no sign the odds ratio differs between buckets.

    Answer: no detectable home advantage once grid is held fixed.

  9. Q2: why cluster the standard errors by driver, and what does conditioning on grid hide?

    The same driver appears in many rows, so rows are not independent; clustering stops the test from being overconfident. Conditioning on grid removes any home effect that works through qualifying (a driver qualifying better at home). That is intended, because the question compares drivers who started from similar positions.

    Task 10.3 and the Q2 answer

    Four categories: finished (34), mechanical (74), accident/driver (12), regulatory/other (19). Mechanical means the label names a failed car component or system. Instructor ruling on Piazza: Puncture, Tyre, Tyre puncture, Fire and Safety belt are mechanical; Refuelling is not. Out of fuel, Fuel and Fuel rig stay with Refuelling.   11. Q3: how is the rate defined, and what is the answer?

    Mechanical retirements divided by race starts, per constructor and era, for constructors with at least 40 starts in the era (about one season of a two-car team).

    • 2014–2021: Lotus F1 20.0% (15 of 75), Toro Rosso 16.5%, Renault 14.1%.

  12. Q3: how did you handle renamed teams, and is the ranking stable between eras?

    DEC-014 merges lineages (Toro Rosso / AlphaTauri / RB, Force India / Racing Point / Aston Martin, Lotus F1 / Renault / Alpine, Sauber / Alfa Romeo). Counting the generic “Retired” label as mechanical barely moves 2014–2021 (ρ = 0.97) but reshuffles the middle of 2022–2024 (ρ = 0.66).

    Tasks 11.4–11.5

  13. What two rules does every feature follow?
    • Nothing from the current race. Outcomes enter only through aggregates of earlier races: rows sorted by date and shifted by one race before any rolling or expanding window.
    • Missing stays missing. No imputation in Step 4. That is a preprocessing choice, fitted on training rows only, in Step 5.

    Step 4 intro, cell 65

  14. How do you guarantee that shift(1) means “strictly earlier”?

    Rows are sorted by date, and two assertions hold: no two races share a date, and no driver has two rows on one date. Then shifting by one within a driver removes exactly the current race, never a same-day race.

    Task 12.1

  15. How are the standings features built without leaking the current race?

    Ergast stores standings as they stood after each race. The table for round r − 1 is therefore the pre-race state of round r: the code adds 1 to round and merges on season, round and driver (or constructor). It asserts the standings date is before the race date. In round 1, or a driver's first race of a season, points share is 0 and rank is missing.

    Task 12.5

  16. Why are points features expressed as shares (DEC-015)?

    A win was worth 8–10 points until 2009 and 25 from 2010. Raw points would mean something different in training than in validation and test. So constructor form is a share of all points awarded in its last 5 races, and standings points are a share of the championship leader's points.

    Tasks 12.4–12.5

  17. Why are there no sprint features (DEC-016)?

    The first sprint was in 2021. The training seasons (up to 2019) contain no sprint rows at all (validation 57, test 283), and a model cannot learn from a column that is empty in everything it trains on. The sprint group is removed, leaving 21 features in 6 groups.

    Task 12.7

  18. Explain gap_to_pole, q3_reached and teammate_grid_diff.
    • gap_to_pole: best of Q1–Q3 divided by the fastest time in that race, minus 1, in %. Above 100% is a recording error and set to missing (one row, 1995 Japanese GP, 1,002.64 s). DEC-017.
    • q3_reached: 1 if a Q3 time was set, defined only for races that had a Q3 session (from 2006); missing otherwise, not 0.
    • teammate_grid_diff: grid minus the mean grid of the same constructor's other cars in that race; negative means ahead; missing for a one-car entry.

    Tasks 12.2–12.3

  19. What does circuit_historical_podium_rate measure, and why per driver (DEC-018)?

    The driver's own podium rate in earlier races at this circuit. Averaged across all drivers, a circuit's podium rate is just 3 divided by the field size, so it would add nothing. It is missing on a driver's first visit (31% of training rows).

    Task 12.4

  20. How did you prove the features do not leak? Walk through both tests.
    • Brute force: for 300 random rows, the 6 history features are recomputed with slow, obvious code that only sees rows with date < date_t. All match model_df.
    • Corruption: for 20 random races, every outcome from that race onward is replaced by garbage (finishing order shuffled, +100 points, podium flipped, later standings altered) and the whole table is rebuilt. All 21 features of that race come out identical.
    • Control: a rolling rate computed without shift(1) is caught in 20 of 20 races, so the test can actually detect leakage.

    Task 13

  21. Which features are strongest on their own, and which overlap?

    On training seasons only. ROC-AUC used alone: quali_position 0.905 and gap_to_pole 0.887, but only on the 37% of rows where they exist; grid 0.884 on every row; form and standings 0.79–0.84. pit_start, is_home_race, round and missing_quali_flag are about 0.50. Overlaps: grid vs. quali_position ρ = 0.98; rank vs. points within each standings table ρ = −0.92.

    Tasks 14.3–14.5

  22. Why a 5-race rolling window?

    The three driver-form features together, in a logistic regression on training seasons: ROC-AUC 0.836 with 3 races, 0.843 with 5, 0.846 with 10. Ten alone scores higher for the rolling rate, but with the career rate alongside, 10 adds almost nothing over 5, and 5 reacts faster to a change of form.

    Task 14.6

  23. Why does every feature-vs-target view use the training seasons only?

    So that no choice about the features is informed by the validation or test seasons. Otherwise their scores would no longer be honest.

Expected questions

  1. Why are driverId and constructorId not one-hot encoded (DEC-020)?

    Many held-out rows belong to someone the model never saw: 13.7% of validation and 21.9% of test rows have a driver absent from training (15.6% and 20.0% for constructors). Their one-hot columns would be all zeros. Form, standings and circuit-history features describe the same thing in a way that also works for a newcomer. Every feature is already numeric or 0/1, so no encoder is needed.

    Task 15.2

  2. Describe the preprocessing pipeline.
    • Qualifying fallback: where quali_position is missing, use the grid slot (a FunctionTransformer).
    • 16 continuous features: median imputation, then standard scaling.
    • 5 binary flags: a missing flag is filled with 0; flags are not scaled.

    Built with ColumnTransformer inside a Pipeline, always fitted on training rows only. It is used by logistic regression, the forest and the network; LightGBM takes the raw features.

    Task 16.1 · DEC-020

  3. Is filling quali_position from the grid a form of leakage?

    No. It works row by row and learns nothing from other rows, so it can be applied to any split. The grid slot is itself known before the race. Before 2003, qualifying position and grid agree in 98% of the rows that have both, so the filled values are sensible.

    Tasks 16.1, 17

  4. Why are confidence intervals computed by resampling whole races?

    Rows in a race are not independent: exactly three of them are podiums. Resampling single rows would treat them as independent and make the intervals too narrow. Resampling the 39 validation races (or 68 test races) 500 times, with the same resamples for every model, gives honest intervals for each PR-AUC and for the difference between two models.

    Tasks 16.2, 21.2

  5. What did the training-window experiment decide (DEC-019)?

    Train on all seasons, with the grid fallback. For logistic regression the window barely matters (validation PR-AUC 0.727 on all seasons, 0.719 from 1994, 0.725 from 2003, 0.729 from 2006), all inside the noise of 39 races. Median imputation vs. grid fallback makes no difference (0.7269 vs. 0.7262). Gradient boosting gets worse with less data (0.713 on all seasons, 0.665 from 2006). So no rows are dropped.

    Task 17

  6. Experiment 1: what happens if the preprocessor is fitted on all rows?

    That is leakage, but here it changes nothing measurable (PR-AUC Δ −0.0001). Validation and test are only 8.7% of the rows, so medians barely move and standard deviations shift 1–2% (7% for gap_to_pole); a linear model absorbs that. The pipeline is still fitted on training rows only: it costs nothing, keeps scores honest by construction, and the same shortcut would matter far more for something that looks at the target, such as target encoding.

    Task 19.1

  7. Experiment 2: impute then scale, or scale then impute?

    The score does not change (Δ −0.0001), but the transformed values do. With imputation first, the scaler sees many copies of the median and learns a smaller standard deviation: 1.58 instead of 2.57 for gap_to_pole (64% missing), so its real values end up about 63% further from zero. Logistic regression compensates with a smaller coefficient. Impute → scale is kept: it is conventional and guarantees mean 0 and SD 1 on training.

    Task 19.2

  8. How do validation and test differ from training after preprocessing?

    Modern grids stop at 20 cars, so field_size sits 0.95 training SDs below the training mean, and grid and rank features have no long tail. round is 0.4–0.7 SD above, because seasons now have more races. This shift is real and is the reason validation uses recent seasons.

    Task 18

  9. How can the grid ≤ 3 rule have a PR-AUC, and what is the bar it sets?

    Its score is minus the grid slot, so pole ranks first and PR-AUC can be computed; the yes/no rule is a cut at slot 3. It reaches 0.648 on validation (F1 0.695) and 0.647 on test. Logistic regression with all 21 features reaches 0.726, so the other 20 features add about 0.08 on validation. Sweeping C from 0.01 to 100 changes nothing.

    Task 22 · DEC-023

  10. Why is every model's training score lower than its validation score? Isn't that suspicious?

    The training seasons (1950–2019) are harder: larger, more variable fields, a lower podium rate (13.4% vs. 15.1%), and mostly no qualifying data. So a training score below validation is expected. Overfitting would show as a training score far above validation, as with the untuned forest (1.000 vs. 0.685).

    Task 22 result, Task 23

  11. What did tuning do to the random forest?

    The untuned forest grows full trees and overfits (training PR-AUC 1.000, validation 0.685). The search over depth, leaf size and features per split picks very shallow trees: max_depth = 4, min_samples_leaf = 5, max_features = 0.8, validation 0.735. Across the 20 configurations the mean validation PR-AUC ranges from 0.668 to 0.738: the search matters more than the model type.

    Task 23

  12. Why LightGBM, and how is it configured (DEC-024)?

    It reads missing values natively (each split learns a branch for them), does not depend on feature scale, and is supported by SHAP's exact TreeExplainer. It is fitted on raw features with balanced class weights and n_jobs = 1 so a run gives the same trees on any machine. Chosen configuration: 100 trees of 8 leaves, learning rate 0.03, 70% of features per tree; validation 0.743 (untuned 0.702). Feeding it imputed and scaled features instead makes no difference (−0.004, interval includes zero).

    Task 24

  13. Why is every configuration scored on five seeds (DEC-037)?

    Near the top of a search, configurations differ by less than the seed-to-seed noise, so a single-seed search picks whichever got a lucky seed. After DEC-034 removed 157 rows, a single-seed search switched LightGBM to a configuration that scored 0.746 on seed 42 but averaged 0.732, against 0.742 for the one it replaced. Ranking by the mean over seeds 42, 0, 1, 2, 3 fixes that; the final model is then fitted once with seed 42.

    Tasks 21, 23–25 · decision log DEC-037

  14. Describe the neural network and how it was chosen (DEC-026).

    A Keras feed-forward network on the 21 preprocessed features: ReLU hidden layers with dropout, one sigmoid output, binary cross-entropy with balanced class weights (a podium row counts 6.4 times as much), Adam at 0.001, batch 256. Early stopping on validation PR-AUC with patience 10, keeping the best epoch. Eight architectures (32, 64, 32→16, 64→32; dropout 0.2 or 0.4) × 5 seeds; chosen 64 → 32 with dropout 0.2, validation 0.733. The seed moves the score as much as the architecture (0.726–0.738).

    Task 25

  15. The network stops on validation PR-AUC. Doesn't that use validation for training?

    It uses validation to choose one number, the stopping epoch, in the same way validation chooses hyperparameters for the other models. It makes the network's validation score slightly flattering, which the notebook states. The fair score is the test score, which validation never touched.

    Task 25 result

  16. Does class weighting help (DEC-027)?

    It does not change the ranking measurably: without weights the PR-AUC moves by −0.016 to +0.002, and every interval includes zero. But it roughly doubles the outputs: the mean predicted value on validation is 0.31–0.34 with weights against a true podium rate of 0.151 (0.14–0.15 without). So the outputs are scores, not probabilities. That is why the tuned thresholds sit above 0.5 and why Step 9 recalibrates.

    Task 26

  17. How are the decision thresholds chosen, and what is “top 3 per race” (DEC-028)?

    For each model, the threshold with the highest F1 on validation: 0.59 (logistic), 0.76 (forest), 0.75 (LightGBM), 0.74 (network). At 0.5 the F1 would be 0.60–0.64, below the grid rule. The F1 curves are flat between about 0.6 and 0.85, so the exact peak is partly chance. Top 3 per race names the three highest-scoring drivers in each race: it needs no tuning and uses the fact that every race has exactly three podium places.

    Task 27.1

  18. How was the final model chosen, and why not refit on train + validation (DEC-029, DEC-030)?

    The rule was fixed beforehand: highest validation PR-AUC among the learned models, which is LightGBM (0.743). None of the four is measurably better than logistic regression on validation (all intervals include zero). The models are not refit on train + validation, so the model described by the validation numbers is exactly the one scored on test.

    Tasks 27.2, 28 intro

  19. What happened on the test seasons?

    Test PR-AUC: grid rule 0.647, logistic regression 0.712, forest 0.707, LightGBM 0.709, network 0.720. The rule is 0.064 below logistic regression (−0.118 to −0.011). No learned model differs measurably from logistic regression. From validation to test, the tree models chosen as best of 20 or 40 configurations fall most (forest 0.028, LightGBM 0.034); logistic regression falls 0.015 and the network 0.013.

    Tasks 28.1–28.2

  20. Do the thresholds and the top-3 rule transfer to the test seasons?

    The thresholds predict too many podiums on test: 3.75 per race for LightGBM and up to 5.2 for logistic regression, against 3 real ones. Recall rises and precision falls; test F1 is 0.670–0.684 against 0.649 for the grid rule. LightGBM finds 157 podiums, misses 47 and raises 98 false alarms. Top 3 per race gets about two of the three podium places right (1.94–2.00 per race), the same as the grid rule (1.96). The models' advantage is in ranking the whole field, not in naming exactly three.

Expected questions

  1. What is the ablation protocol, and why run it on LightGBM (DEC-031)?

    LightGBM is the selected model and takes raw features, so a group can be dropped without touching an imputer or scaler. Only the columns change: same hyperparameters, training seasons and class weights, nothing retuned. Each variant is scored on validation and test, with a race-bootstrap 95% interval on the difference and the same difference averaged over 10 seeds. Nothing is chosen from the results.

    Task 29

  2. Why the 10-seed check?

    Each tree is offered a random 70% of the features (colsample_bytree = 0.7). Removing columns changes that draw, so part of any difference is chance and not the removed group. The seed alone moves the full model's PR-AUC between 0.740 and 0.744 on validation and 0.707 and 0.717 on test.

    Task 29.1

  3. What does leaving one group out show?

    No single group is needed: every 95% interval includes zero on validation and test. Grid has the most consistent cost (about −0.017 on validation, −0.018 on test, −0.020 across seeds). Qualifying looks important on validation (−0.015) but helps on test (+0.014): noise. Driver form, constructor form and standings cost under 0.01; context costs nothing.

    Task 30

  4. So are the features useless? Why remove whole blocks?

    No. Leave-one-out measures what a group adds on top of all the others. Grid and qualifying both say where the car starts; driver form, constructor form and standings all say how driver and team have been doing. Remove one and the model reads the same information from its neighbour. Removing whole blocks shows the information itself: without the starting position the model loses 0.085 on validation and 0.121 on test; without past results 0.064 and 0.031; without context nothing.

    Tasks 30–31

    Starting position alone scores 0.670 / 0.667 (validation / test), close to the grid rule. Past results alone score 0.652 / 0.591, so they transfer worse to 2022–2024, after the ground-effect regulation change (consistent with a rule change, though two splits cannot prove it). Context alone scores 0.389 / 0.273.

    Task 31.2

  6. Context adds nothing. Why didn't you remove it?

    The model was fixed in Step 6, before the test seasons were scored. Removing context now would change the model based on test results, for no gain in score. All 21 features stay (DEC-031); a simpler model without context is listed as future work.

    Task 31 result · Future Work

  7. How does permutation importance work here, and what does it show?

    Shuffle a feature's values across the validation rows (50 times), which breaks its link to the outcome while keeping its distribution, and measure the drop in PR-AUC of the fitted model. Groups and blocks are shuffled together, with one row order for all their columns. grid alone costs 0.229 (0.148–0.302); no other single feature is clearly above zero. Blocks: starting position 0.390, past results 0.167, context 0. Single drops sum to 0.311, but shuffling everything costs 0.586: overlapping features cover for each other.

    Task 32

  8. What is a SHAP value here, and in what unit?

    SHAP splits one prediction into a contribution per feature. For trees it is exact: base value plus the 21 contributions equals the model's raw output (asserted). The unit is log-odds of the weighted score, not a probability. Base value −1.092 log-odds (score 0.251); the decision threshold 0.747 is +1.084 log-odds.

    Task 33.1

  9. What does SHAP show about how the model uses its features?

    • The grid effect is a staircase: slots 1–3 all about +1.6, then +1.34, +0.96, +0.67, crossing zero between slots 9 and 10; slots 11–14 about −0.4 and 15 onward about −1.0.

    Tasks 33.1–33.3

  10. Why must the SHAP value of q3_reached not be read literally?

    Task 33.3

  11. SHAP, permutation and retraining give different sizes for the same block. Which is right?

    All three agree on the order (starting position, then past results, then context) and answer different questions. Permutation asks what the fitted model loses when a block is scrambled: it still trusts the scrambled values, so the loss is large (0.390). Retraining asks what a new model loses without the block: it leans on what is left, so the loss is smaller (0.085). For the grid group alone: 0.279 by permutation but 0.017 by retraining, because a retrained model takes the same information from qualifying.

    Task 33.4

  12. How were the two local cases chosen, and what do they show?

    By rule, from validation rows with no missing feature: the podium finisher with the lowest score, and the pole-sitter off the podium with the highest score. Both are from the 2020 Italian GP. Gasly (AlphaTauri, grid 10, score 0.127) won: every SHAP bar points down, a midfield profile. Hamilton (Mercedes, pole, score 0.915) finished 7th: pole alone adds about +1.65.     Task 34

  13. How does LIME work here, and why does it fail on the Gasly case?

    LIME samples 5,000 artificial rows around the case from a background of the 4,027 training rows with no missing value (2006–2019), scores them with the model and fits a small linear model; its weights are the explanation, in score units. For Hamilton it works: R² 0.82, top feature grid as in SHAP, the same on all 5 seeds. For Gasly it fails: R² 0.05, its top feature is pit_start, which no tree uses (SHAP 0), and the top feature changes across seeds. pit_start = 1 is in only 7 background rows, so noise looks like an effect. SHAP is the method to rely on; LIME is a second opinion, quoted only with its R².

    Task 35

  14. What must not be concluded from the explanations?
    • Importance is not causation: grid summarises car, driver and setup speed that weekend; putting a slow car on pole would not make it a contender.
    • Credit between near-duplicates (grid vs. quali_position, ρ = 0.98) is arbitrary; quote importance per block.
    • They describe this weighted model on 2020–2021, not other seasons, and its scores are not probabilities.

    Cell 182 · DEC-032

  15. Why calibrate, how, and on which data (DEC-033)?

    Class weights push the raw score up (mean 0.32 against a podium rate of 0.15), so 0.5 would wrongly read as “a 50% chance”. Platt scaling fits a one-input logistic regression from the model's log-odds to the outcome, on validation only: probability = sigmoid(1.348 × log-odds − 1.838). Two parameters, so 775 rows suffice. It is monotone, so the ranking, PR-AUC and every yes/no prediction stay the same. On test (check only): mean probability 0.161 vs. podium rate 0.151; Brier 0.117 → 0.069; top bin 0.70 predicted vs. 0.70 observed.

    Task 36

  16. The function says “podium” at a probability of 0.41. Why not 0.5?

    The decision threshold is still the F1-optimal score from validation (0.747), which maps to a calibrated probability of 0.407. It is the point where F1 was highest, not a “more likely than not” rule. On test it predicts about 3.75 podiums per race against 3 real ones.

    Task 36.1 · DEC-028

  17. How does pre_race_features() avoid leaking the race it predicts?

    It does not look up a row in model_df. It calls the same build_feature_table() as training on every race strictly before this one, plus this race's entry list with finishing order, points and podium set to missing. The grid and qualifying passed in replace the recorded ones. Check: 36 validation and test rows (including 7 pit-lane starts and 1 without qualifying) are rebuilt with identical features and identical scores.

  18. How does predict_podium() handle missing qualifying, a pit-lane start, an unknown driver and bad input?
    • quali_info=None leaves the qualifying features missing; LightGBM routes them down its learned missing-value branch (seen in about 14,000 training rows). Norris at Abu Dhabi 2024: 0.756 with full qualifying, 0.688 with position only, 0.664 with none. Over the 2024 season, removing qualifying moves PR-AUC 0.694 → 0.692 and flips 8 of 476 predictions.
    • grid = 0 is recoded as in cleaning: one slot behind the last car, pit_start = 1.
    • A driver not in the entry list is added in the team of their last race (Ocon at slot 10: 0.049).
    • Unknown race or driver, a non-integer grid, or an unknown qualifying key raise a ValueError with a message.

  19. The grid what-if rises again at slots 19–20. What does that mean?

    For Norris with no qualifying data, the probability falls from 0.66 (slots 1–2) to 0.45 at slot 5 (the last predicted podium) and 0.10 at slots 15–18, then rises to 0.15 at 19, 20 and the pit lane. The training data does not support that: strong teams' cars from slot 19 or further back reached the podium in 4.2% of 288 cases, against 6.5% from slots 13–16. Such cars are rare, so the model's output there should not be trusted. It is also a what-if on the model, not the race: only this driver's slot changes.
